# Music-video looks of 2001-2009: emo / pop-punk and hip-hop (research only)

**Result: the two scenes share a small toolkit (high-contrast or monochrome grades, film-damage textures, glow
and star glints, fisheye and widescreen framing, CRT/camcorder/webcam degradation and, at the very end of the
decade, datamosh), and almost every look the owner asked for maps onto a VEGAS-named FX, a short FX chain or a
transition.** The sources describe concepts, directors and post houses far more often than grades, so several
items on the starting list are marked *not source-backed* below rather than asserted. Nothing in this note is
product code. Artist, director and video names appear only in the research narrative and the reference lists;
none of the proposed Milan names uses them.

## 1. Scope and method

- **Read, not downloaded.** Only web pages were read: Wikipedia pages of individual videos and directors, trade
  and retrospective articles (American Cinematographer, Motionographer, Vice, The Ringer, DJBooth, Fortune, AV
  Club, CDM), Microsoft's archived Movie Maker SDK pages, and VEGAS user forums (DV Info Net, Creative Cow). No
  video, frame, image or media file was downloaded or watched; every look below is described from text. Nothing
  is quoted beyond a few words; claims carry numbered references (Sources, at the end).
- **Pages that could not be read.** Kerrang, LBB Online, Studio Daily and Post Magazine refused or timed out on
  automated fetches. A claim that rests only on a search-engine excerpt of such a page is marked *(excerpt)*;
  treat those as weaker.
- **Local, read-only VEGAS check.** VEGAS Pro 15 is installed on this machine (see `VEG_FORMAT.md`). Its OFX
  resource and preset files were read for exact parameter labels, choice lists and preset values (Appendix A,
  **[L1]**, **[L2]**), and the UI strings of the older DXT plug-in DLLs were listed to confirm the labels predate
  the OFX port (**[L3]**). VEGAS was not started and nothing was changed. VEGAS's online help (now hosted by Boris
  FX) explains how transitions are applied but has no per-plug-in parameter pages [61], so these local files are
  the only complete "as documented" source for parameter names.
- **Period.** 2001-2009, with two exceptions kept for lineage: Hype Williams's late-1990s fisheye and widescreen
  work, and post-2009 forum threads that record how VEGAS users built these looks.

## 2. Scene A: emo, pop-punk, post-hardcore (2001-2009)

### 2.1 How the videos were made

The scene had two economies. Early label videos were cheap: one director who made many of them recalls label
budgets of roughly three to eight thousand dollars and early shoots on consumer-grade digital video cameras,
rising to about $30,000 for a 2006 breakout clip and about $80,000 for its follow-up [18]. At the top end,
major-label videos were shot on film by established directors: Marc Webb, an editor before he directed, made most
of the early-to-mid-decade My Chemical Romance, AFI and Good Charlotte clips [8][9][15][23], and Samuel Bayer,
who won the 2005 MTV cinematography award for a Green Day clip, shot "Welcome to the Black Parade" and "Famous
Last Words" back to back in 2006 on a set built in a hangar [10][11]. Film-originated videos were graded in
telecine; a colorist won an award for the Black Parade grade and had graded Bayer's Green Day work before
*(excerpt)* [13]. A 2004 trade piece has a director asking the telecine colorist for a low-contrast, grainy,
smoky image that graders called a "print look" *(excerpt)* [24].

### 2.3 Optics, motion and cutting

The sources describe this scene by concept and performance, not by camera trick: a funeral turned into a
choreographed dance in which the corpse rises and grabs the camera [8]; a fake teen-movie trailer [9]; a boy with
antlers in a surreal fable [16]; a school-hallway performance intercut with a story [17]; masks and red curtains
as one director's recurring device [18]; a singer walking toward camera while the room spins through a lifetime
[19]. Performance intercut with narrative is the common cutting grammar [17][18]. Crash zooms, whip pans, flash
frames, speed ramps and stutter cuts are **not** described for any emo video in the sources read; they are
plausible but unverified for this scene.

## 3. Scene B: hip-hop, rap, crunk, R&B-rap (2001-2009)

### 3.1 How the videos were made

At the top end this was a film-and-post-house world. A July 2001 American Cinematographer profile of Hype
Williams describes extreme wide-angle and fisheye lenses (8 mm and 10 mm early on, later anamorphic Panavision
24-35 mm), shooting almost everything widescreen and letterboxed so no 4:3 version had to be framed, a hard-edged
light sweeping across faces in time with the music in one clip, colour carried by sets and wardrobe, and in
spring 2001 a move to Sony 24p HD cameras [27]. Effects-heavy clips went through post houses: the 2007 "Stronger"
video had VFX by RhinoFX, including Inferno work, roto, smoke animation and glowing starbursts, and West distorted
New York footage to look like a CRT broadcast [36]. Dave Meyers's Missy Elliott videos relied on compositing (a
head pulled from a body, a mannequin head composited with Elliott for the bee scene) and wire work [31][32][33]
[34]. At the low end, 2006-2007 YouTube uploads were self-shot on cheap cameras [47], and the 2009 datamosh was
done by re-encoding AVIs and deleting keyframes, then rendering through Final Cut [1].

### 3.3 Optics, motion and cutting

- **Fisheye.** The defining hip-hop optic: it magnifies the centre and bends realistic sets into something
  cartoonish [27][29]. In an NLE this is Spherize with a positive amount plus a slight scale-up.
- **Split frames.** Hype Williams is credited with popularising split-screen formats in the early 2000s [28];
  "Hey Ya!" used motion-control photography so one performer plays all eight band members [35].
- **Slow motion and frame-rate play.** "Flashing Lights" (2008) is shot in slow motion in an empty desert [37];
  a 1997 Hype Williams clip uses doubled frame rates [29]. Speed changes are supported; ramp curves are not
  described.
- **Rhythmic light.** Light sweeping across faces in time with the music [27] is the source-backed ancestor of a
  "club strobe" look; explicit strobe or flash-frame cutting is not described in the sources read.
- **Choppy, stuttering edit.** "Stronger" is cut with choppy cuts and distorted scenes [36].
- **Compositing gags.** Spit flying across a room, detached heads, a dance on a wall [31][34].

## 4. Bedroom editors and NLE built-ins of the era

**Tools and dates.** Windows Movie Maker shipped in Windows Me (2000) and XP (2001); version 2.0 (Nov 2002)
gave XP users 60 transitions and 37 effects, Vista's 6.0 (2007) had 63 transitions and 49 effects with Direct3D,
and Windows Live Movie Maker (2009) was a new, simpler program [50]. VEGAS went from Vegas 4 (Feb 2003, Sonic
Foundry) to Sony Vegas 4.0e (Nov 2003), 5 (2004), 6 (2005), 7 (2006), Pro 8 (2007), Pro 9 (2009) and Pro 10
(2010, first with OpenFX) [51]. Vegas Pro 9 users in July 2009 report the new built-ins Defocus, Glint, Fill
Light, Soft Contrast, Starburst and Rays [54]. Effects-heavy anime music videos ("FX editing") are described as
made in Sony Vegas and After Effects [52]; AMVs spread through AnimeMusicVideos.org and, from about 2005, YouTube,
with record labels pushing takedowns from late 2005 [52]. Andrew Kramer's Video Copilot After Effects tutorials
began around 2005 and were featured on Creative Cow *(excerpt)* [60].

**Movie Maker's transition vocabulary** (exact names from the SDK XML) [48]: Bars; Bow Tie (horizontal,
vertical); Checkerboard, Across; Circle; Circles; Diagonal (Box Out, Cross Out, Down Right); Diamond; Dissolve;
Dissolve, Rough; Dissolve, Vertical; Eye; Fade; Fan (In, Out, In Vertical); Filled V (four directions); Flip,
Right; Heart; Inset (four corners); Iris; Keyhole; Page Curl (Up Right, Up Left); Pixelate Transition;
Rectangle; Reveal (Down, Right); Roll; Shatter (In, Right, Up Left, Up Right); Shrink, In; Slide; Slide, Up
Center; Spin; Split (horizontal, vertical); Star, 5 Points; Stars, 5 Points; Sweep (In, Out); Swinging Door,
Bottom; Wheel, 4 Spokes; Whirlwind; Whirlwind from top; Wipe (narrow, normal, wide); Zig Zag. Parameter names
worth copying: Page Curl uses `PageCurlA`, `ShowABack`, `VertexVelocity`, `FadeStartA`; Shatter uses
`MaxParticles` (64-700), `ScatterDirection`, `ParticleSpeed`, `ParticleSpinSpeed`, `PulseMode`; wipes use a
`Mesh`, `Edge` (Soft/Sharp), `GradientWidth` (0.05-0.6) and a SMPTE wipe number.

**Movie Maker's effect vocabulary** [49]: Film Age (Old, Older, Oldest), Film Grain, Sepia Tone, Grayscale,
Posterize (5 levels), Threshold (Posterize with 1 level), Pixelate (size 1 to 40 across the clip), Blur, Sharpen,
Edge Detection, Watercolor (Kuwahara), Hue cycle, 3D Ripple, Mirror (horizontal, vertical), Rotate, Spin 360,
Ease In/Out and pan/zoom presets, fades from/to black or white, Brightness up/down, Dream, Speed Up (2x) and Slow
Down (0.5x). Film Age is the most useful recipe on record: noise, splotch, line and lint frequencies rise from
0.25/0.05/0.10/0.05 (Old) to 1.00/0.20/0.30/0.40 (Oldest), with posterize levels 128 down to 5, shake 0 to 0.20
and blur 3 to 7 [49]. That is a ready-made "how old" axis for Milan's Film Effects presets.

**VEGAS recipes recorded by users.**
- *Old film (2007):* Film Effects for dust, scratches and hair (keyframe Amount), the separate Film Grain FX for
  grain, Sepia for tint, a brief TV Simulator flash, a slight Glow, a velocity envelope around 140 % and a slow
  camera shutter. The same thread records that Film Effects particles did not animate until Vegas 6.0d [53].
- *Gate weave:* Film Effects' Jitter gives the small jumps of old film; the same 2010 thread points to an older
  community "Quake" script for shaking footage [56].
- *Zoom-through transition:* a zoom keyed together with Radial Blur, or the Cross Effect transition in its zoom
  mode applied to one side only [55].
- *Punch pulse:* Pinch/Punch from the Medium Punch preset (Amount 0.500), ramped 0.1-0.4 over four frames, held
  six frames, ramped back, with smooth keyframe interpolation [59].
- *Radial Blur in and out:* key only the Strength slider [60].
- *Rolling TV:* TV Simulator's Vertical Sync rolls the picture top to bottom as it goes from 1 to 0, and it cannot
  go negative [58].
- *Stutter transitions:* VEGAS's Transition Progress envelope holds, reverses or repeats a transition [61].

**What is not documented.** No source read shows 2001-2009 bedroom editors using light-leak or film-burn
overlay footage; the overlay-pack culture appears later, and the sources only explain that burns and leaks began
as accidental exposure of film to light [63]. The cube look comes from Premiere's "Cube Spin" [64]; VEGAS 15 has
no cube transition (Appendix A), and Movie Maker's 3D set is Flip, Roll, Spin, Shrink and Page Curl [48].

## 5. Datamosh

**What it is.** Compressed video stores complete pictures (I-frames, keyframes) only now and then; the frames
between them (P-frames; one tutorial calls them delta or "d" frames) store how blocks of the previous picture move,
plus small corrections [1][6]. Datamoshing breaks that chain on purpose. Deleting I-frames makes the next shot's
motion data push around the *previous* shot's pixels, so one scene appears projected onto, and dragged by, the
movement of the next; duplicating P-frames applies the same motion again and again, so pixels smear and bloom
along one path [6]. Tutorials disagree on which of the two to call "melt" [6], so Milan should name its modes by
mechanism. The carry-over variant needs a cut to work against; the repeat variant works on any moving shot.
Wikipedia summarises the result as frames interpolated from two separate sources [2].

**History.** Digital artists were datamoshing by about 2005 [5]. Takeshi Murata's *Monster Movie* (2005)
dissolved a 1981 B-movie into digital liquid by breaking its compressed file [3]. In 2006 Bertrand Planes and
Christian Jacquemin transcoded through a modified Xvid-based codec, DivXPrime [2][5]; Sven König, Jacques
Perconte, Paul B. Davis with Paper Rad, and David O'Reilly also worked with the effect [1][2][4]. In February 2009,
within days of each other, Ray Tintori's video for Chairlift's "Evident Utensil" and Nabil's "Welcome to
Heartbreak" made it mainstream [1][4]. Ghost Town Media's account of the latter: work from October 2008 to early
January 2009, some 45-50 versions, a green-screen shoot with the moshes planned in the edit, an AVI re-encode at a
higher data rate so every cut began with an I-frame, deletion of those I-frames (7-8 GB down to about 125 MB), and
a six-hour render back through Final Cut [1]. West released his video early because Chairlift's used the same
technique *(excerpt)* [7].

**Milan's version is an approximation.** Milan does not corrupt codec streams (unrepeatable, decoder-dependent
and unsafe to render). Its **Datamosh** FX (`src/engine/mvfx/temporal.rs`) works on decoded pictures: every
*Keyframe interval* frames of the event, a picture is held as the "keyframe"; for the next *Smear length* frames that
held picture is dragged along by block motion measured between each pair of consecutive source frames (blocks of
*Block size*, a full search over +-*Motion search* on a quarter-resolution luma copy, the smallest move winning ties
so flat areas stay still), and after each step the true frame bleeds back in by *Bleed*. The result is the carry-over
("I-frame deleted") look: the old picture moves with the new motion and slowly dissolves into it. It reads the earlier
frames through the same decoder and frame cache as the preview and is a pure function of the frame number, so a
seek, RAM preview, prerender and render agree. It is a pixel-domain smear, not codec-level moshing: no codec
residuals, no real macroblock artefacts, the same result every time (which a real mosh is not), and the motion of
a velocity-ramped event is taken on the source-frame grid. The repeat ("P-frame duplicated") variant and moshing
across an edit point between two events are not built; the **Glitch** transition stands in at cuts.

## 6. Light leaks, film burns and dust: procedural vs footage

Milan's Light Leak, Film Burn and Film Effects (dust, hair, scratches, flicker, jitter) are generated
procedurally (noise fields, gradients and particles evaluated per frame), so they need no media, render at any
resolution and can be keyframed like VEGAS's Film Effects. Real scanned light-leak and burn footage, and any
user-supplied texture packs, belong in Milan's **Overlays** tab (`src/overlays.rs`), which already handles packs,
default blends (Screen, Add, Overlay, Multiply, keyed) and a generated sample pack with a grain and a light-leak
clip. The two routes complement each other: procedural for speed and control, overlays for organic texture.
